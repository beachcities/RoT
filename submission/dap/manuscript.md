# Return on Token: An Indicator Linking Data Self-Description to AI Inference Token Consumption

*Working draft for Data & Policy (standard track, Research Article). Derived from Frozen Public Release v0.2.0 (`d483d6b`). The frozen files under `paper/` are not modified by this derivative. Front-matter statements (Abstract, Policy Significance Statement, Keywords, disclosure statements) are maintained in [`statements.md`](statements.md). Notation: this derivative uses "RoT" throughout; the frozen paper's "ROT" is unchanged on the frozen side.*

## 1. Introduction

Hand an AI agent a spreadsheet from a government portal, or a file from inside an organisation, and watch the execution log. A reasoning model emits a long chain of thought, spending tokens at a furious rate. A fair reading of such logs suggests that a non-trivial share of the effort is not going into the problem the model was asked to solve, but into premises the data never stated: what does this abbreviated column name refer to? Is this figure in millions of yen, or in thousands? Why does the format differ from the same period last year? The model cycles through hypothesis and verification, wanders, and retries — filling in what the data could have said. Inference-time scaling has made it possible to externalise reasoning as tokens generated at runtime; what it may also have produced is a structural form of idling, in which expensive, power-hungry compute is spent guessing at data that does not introduce itself. Had the data said, in one line, "this column is revenue, in millions of yen," that expenditure would not have been needed — and on the tasks we construct below, we measure how large the difference can be.

This paper makes four contributions. First, it defines Return on Token (RoT) — outcome obtained per total tokens invested, with recursive and discarded search included in the denominator — and states explicitly which components of the accompanying self-description hypothesis are measured and which remain hypotheses (Section 3). Second, it reports measurements on a minimal benchmark that varies the self-description of data over ten levels while holding two synthetic tasks constant: a boundary in consumption appeared at the same position in every system measured, with step sizes reported per measurement series (Section 4). Third, it reads the intermediate reasoning of one fully open system at the levels around that boundary, and reports a first trial of deriving candidate data requirements from the reasoning of failed attempts (Sections 4.3 and 4.5). Fourth, it draws out, as explicitly conditional policy hypotheses, what such measurements could mean for prioritising open-data investment (Section 6). Section 5 gathers the limitations in one place; transfer to real data is the largest of them and has not been measured.

A note on the name. "Return on Token(s)" is used in several contexts independently of this paper, mostly, as far as we can tell, to mean a financial rate of return on token spend. The RoT proposed here is a different quantity — a measurable ratio of outcome to total tokens consumed, with recursive tokens in the denominator — so both the formula and the object differ.

## 2. Background and related work

**Inference-time scaling and its cost.** Chain-of-thought prompting showed that eliciting intermediate reasoning steps as generated text improves task performance (Wei et al. 2022), and reinforcement-learning-trained reasoning models have since made long generated reasoning a standard operating mode rather than a prompting technique (DeepSeek-AI 2025). The cost side of this shift has been measured: the AI Energy Score comparisons report output-token multipliers of 300–800 and energy multipliers of 150–700 for the same models with reasoning enabled versus disabled (Luccioni and Gamazaychikov 2025). Market projections point the same direction at the aggregate level: Goldman Sachs projects a twenty-four-fold growth in token consumption between 2026 and 2030, and Gartner expects consumption growth to outpace the fall in inference unit costs, so that total inference spending rises (Goldman Sachs Research 2026; Gartner 2026). Token consumption is thus becoming a measurable, growing, and policy-relevant resource.

**Model-side efficiency.** A rapidly growing body of work responds by making the model reason more efficiently. "Overthinking" — reasoning far longer than a problem requires — has been documented directly (Chen et al. 2024), and the first structured survey of efficient reasoning organizes the responses: compressing or shortening reasoning chains, imposing token budgets, exiting early, and switching between reasoning regimes (Sui et al. 2025). Engineering control stacks such as the Tokenomics Foundation's five-layer framework extend this logic from silicon to routing (Tokenomics Foundation n.d.). What these approaches share is the side of the system they act on: they intervene on the model and its generation process, while treating the input data — and whatever the model must infer about it — as given.

**Documentation of data.** A separate literature treats the description of data as the object of design. The FAIR principles made machine-actionable metadata a general target for scientific data (Wilkinson et al. 2016), and datasheets brought standardized documentation practice to machine-learning datasets (Gebru et al. 2021). At the same time, field evidence shows that this documentation work is systematically undervalued relative to model work (Sambasivan et al. 2021) — an undervaluation consistent with the economics of complementary investment in general purpose technologies, where the returns to a one-off improvement of a non-rival input accrue to downstream users rather than to the investor (Bresnahan and Trajtenberg 1995). This literature establishes what good description is and why it is underprovided; what it measures is conformance and practice, not what description does to a model's consumption at inference time.

**Open government data: metadata and value.** In the open-government-data literature the same questions appear at portal scale, and empirically. Quarati (2023) assessed roughly 400,000 datasets across national, municipal, and international portals, measuring their usage alongside programmatically assessed metadata quality and examining the relationship between the two; notably, the study did not find a clear positive correlation between better metadata publishing practices and usage — a caution against treating metadata quality as an established driver of use. On the value side, systematic work asks how "high-value datasets" should be determined (Nikiforova et al. 2023), the European Union has fixed a legal list of high-value dataset categories to be published free of charge in machine-readable form (European Commission 2023), and UNESCO's guidance places making data AI-ready among the preparation steps for opening data (UNESCO 2023). Across this line, the value and priority of public data are assessed through publication, usage, quality conformance, and enumerated categories; the consumption of inference compute does not yet appear among the evaluation axes.

**Where this paper sits.** The four groups above have developed largely separately: the first measures a growing consumption problem, the second manages it from the model side, and the third and fourth build and prioritize data description without measuring its consequences for that consumption. This paper connects them by measurement: it varies the description available to the model and reads the result in tokens per outcome. Within this bounded review, we did not identify prior work that directly measures inference token consumption as a function of whether task-relevant data descriptions are available to the model.

## 3. The RoT indicator and the self-description hypothesis

**3.1 Definition.** We define Return on Token (RoT) as the ratio of outcome obtained to total tokens invested:

$$\text{RoT} = \frac{\text{outcome obtained (task solved, value created)}}{\text{total tokens invested (including recursive and discarded search)}}$$

Both terms need specification. The numerator is the outcome of the run — in the measurements of this paper, simply whether the task was solved. Richer numerators — value created, measured by reproduction cost, substituted labour, or downstream savings — would be needed to connect RoT to macroeconomic value-added statistics, and none of these is standardised; token intensity is further distorted by linguistic and industrial structure, since a Japanese tokeniser does not cost the same as an English one for equivalent content. We leave the numerator's standardisation open and use the minimal, measurable form throughout.

The denominator is deliberately inclusive: it counts every token of the run, including the recursive tokens an agent generates by feeding its own output back into itself, and the search that is ultimately thrown away. The rationale is that the size of the discarded search is itself informative: under the hypothesis examined in this paper, it would reflect the weight of the context the data did not supply. An indicator that excluded failed attempts would hide precisely the expenditure this paper is about.

**3.2 The self-description hypothesis.** We hypothesize that a major determinant of RoT is the self-description of the data: the degree to which the meanings, units, and definitions that a task requires are reachable from where the consumer of the data stands. This is broader, and more demanding, than what "self-describing" usually denotes in data engineering, where the term refers to formats that embed their own schema (JSON, Parquet, Avro and the like). A schema is one means to reachability, not the end: a reference to an external schema that the consumer does not resolve leaves the data, for that consumer, no more self-described than before. The question is not which format was used but whether the required information is present and reachable.

The hypothesis has several components, and they do not share one evidential status; Section 4 reports which have been measured. The component this paper measures directly concerns search: we hypothesize that when required meanings are absent, models expend recursive tokens generating and testing guesses, and that this search diminishes when the description is supplied. A second component received a first, single-point measurement: that description reaching a smaller model can widen the range of tasks it completes — with an observed limit, one description form at which the smaller configuration did not succeed. Three further components remain hypotheses, stated here so that the boundary is explicit. Regularised description could stabilise the leading portion of prompts and so raise cache hit rates; description could tell on the training side, where stated correspondences fold into weights instead of being supplied as context at inference time; and description may be a precondition for *framing* questions, not only answering them — if what the data holds cannot be read off it, a question the data could answer cannot be posed, and the less spare capacity a model has, the more it is confined to what is written. None of these three is measured in this paper.

**3.3 Relation to model-side efficiency.** The approaches surveyed in Section 2 — compressing reasoning chains, imposing token budgets, exiting early — act on the generation process: given the data as it is, they constrain how much the model may spend on it. The intervention this paper examines acts one step earlier, on the data artifact itself, before any model runs. The difference is in the point of intervention, not a claim of superiority, and the two are combinable: a budget caps the search that guessing produces, while, to the extent the hypothesis in 3.2 holds, supplied description diminishes that search. The economic character of the two interventions also differs. A control applied at generation time is exercised on every run and priced in rival compute; a description added to a dataset is a one-off fixed cost on a non-rival good, and, to the extent that its effect transfers across tasks, models, and real data — a transfer this paper does not measure — its benefits would accrue to downstream consumers of that dataset across models and runs. This asymmetry is what later sections develop into a policy question: who should bear a cost whose returns the payer cannot capture.

## 4. Measurements

**4.1 Setup and instruments.** The same tasks were posed against data varied across ten levels of self-description, and token consumption per outcome compared. The levels combine two axes: what is written (from nothing, through meaningful field names and units, to the meanings of codes) and where it sits (inside the record, in a document header, or as an external reference only). Two synthetic tasks were used throughout: a main task requiring the meaning of a code, designed so that it cannot be inferred from other clues, and a control task solvable by inference from company names even when nothing is stated. When a model fails, it is told to reconsider its premises and retry, up to ten attempts; tokens from failed attempts all count toward the denominator. The runtime environment, input data, prompts, and sampling settings are recorded as hashes in the result files, and reproduction steps are published with the benchmark.

Measurements were taken in three series, which we keep separate throughout; numbers are comparable within a series, and we do not aggregate across them.

At every level where the required information was absent from the document, no system solved the main task even once, and every trial ran to the ten-attempt ceiling; at the level where it was present, every system solved it on the first attempt. Total consumption fell by factors of 14 to 30. The boundary appears as a step, not a gradient: the levels below it are not ordered by how much partial description they carry.

Two features of the step matter for what follows. First, the level carrying only an external reference (l5) fell on the same side as stating nothing at all — pointing at a schema and reaching it are not the same thing, the distinction drawn in Section 3.2. Second, the control task shows no step: every system solved it at every level with no substantial difference in consumption. Where a clue exists, the models get there without being told; the boundary appears only where the missing information cannot be inferred. Further systems on the reference route (a thinking-toggle pair and a smaller-capacity model) and the two other series are reported in 4.4.

**4.3 Reading the search: observations from reasoning text.** The three API systems return `reasoning_tokens` as zero and no reasoning text, so their consumption can be measured but not read. Olmo-3-7B-Think, served locally, leaves the contents of its intermediate reasoning as text, and this subsection reports what that text shows on the main task. These are observations of one system's reasoning, and interpretations of it; they are not a causal decomposition of the consumption measured above.

At the external-reference level (l5), the model notices the reference and states that it cannot resolve it ("Since I can't see the external code list (code_list_reference is a url, which I can't access), I need to rely solely on the given data and common sense."). Having said so, it nonetheless spends ten attempts and 279,148 characters of reasoning continuing to guess. At the level stating nothing (l0), it names the gap at the outset ("The problem is, the data doesn't list the industry type for each company.") and rebuilds its own classification on every attempt — the ten answers move 249 → 249 → 264 → 264 → 719 → 943 → 1149 → 943 → 943 → 826, not one guess repeated — across 404,407 characters; partway through it declares its angles exhausted, and the later attempts turn from the data toward inferring what the questioner expects. At the level where the code meanings are stated (l6), the reasoning consists of lookup and addition ("Let me confirm that from the code_definition."), with no hypothesis generation we could identify; the gap between 279,148 characters at l5 and 2,796 at l6 closes on that one lookup. Because characters are no substitute for tokens, the extracted reasoning text was re-counted with the model's own tokeniser: 69,183 tokens at l5 against 1,027 at l6 on the same task. This is an after-the-fact approximation, excluding the enclosing tags and special tokens and therefore erring low; checked against the server-returned consumption, the residual was a median of 4 tokens per attempt (0.06% for Olmo, 0.15% for Qwen, across two tokeniser families).

To put a number on how much of the reasoning concerns the data's gaps, the reasoning of failed attempts was classified sentence by sentence with published, rule-based classifiers (no LLM involved). The share of characters mentioning or guessing at data-side gaps was 16.0% for Olmo and 18.6% for Qwen. The figure moves with the classification rules — up to 23% depending on how sentences touching both sides are treated, down to 1–6% if only explicit statements of absence are counted — and it is a floor for a different quantity than total gap-caused expenditure: a sentence that carries on calculating on top of a guessed correspondence is not captured. Nearly all of it comes from failed attempts at a task built so that a data-side gap is the blocker, so it is not a figure for reasoning in general. One further observation is recorded without resolution: the share of mentions directed at ambiguity in the *question* differed sharply by lineage — 14.3% for Olmo against 3.8% for Qwen — and whether that difference is a habit of how the two write their reasoning, or something substantive, has not been separated.

These observations are consistent with the reading that, on this task, the absent description occasions search and the supplied description makes it unnecessary; the same shape of consumption appeared in the closed systems, where the reasoning cannot be read and attribution is correspondingly harder. We report the pattern as observed, in one open system's text, at the mechanism-visibility level this method affords.

**4.4 Robustness.** Each check below stays within its own series (Table in 4.1); none of the figures here is merged with another series'.

*Toggling thinking (reference route).* Qwen3.5-9B was run twice over the same inputs as every other reference-route run, with thinking enabled and disabled. The boundary appeared at the same place (l5→l6) under both conditions. The step was 25.5× with thinking on (106,403 → 4,165). On the thinking-off side there is no figure that can be compared symmetrically with it: of the six levels below l6, only two (l1, l2) ran unsolved to the ten-attempt ceiling, while the remaining four (l5 among them) were cut off by context-length overruns and excluded from aggregation (the history of this figure is part of the retraction record in 4.6). The one pair comparable on valid measurements is the unsolved trial at l2, run to the full ten attempts under both conditions: total consumption 161,076 with thinking off against 89,166 with it on — roughly 1.8×. The control task was solved on the first attempt at all ten levels under both conditions. Toggling how much the model is allowed to think did not move the boundary. Against expectation, enabling thinking produced fewer errors — context-length overruns numbered four with thinking off and zero with it on; reading the two runs against each other suggests that answers grow long and accumulate in the conversation history when thinking is off, whereas thinking is extracted and not carried forward — but this is a reading of two runs, not a controlled experiment.

*Five-fold repetition (separate local route, A100-80GB).* The three locally served systems (Olmo-3-7B-Think, Qwen3.5-9B thinking on and off) were rerun across all ten levels and both tasks with five repetitions, changing the seed for each sample. The boundary appeared at the same place in all three systems. The steps were 32.6× (Olmo), 22.2× (Qwen, thinking on), and 32.1× (thinking off), as ratios of five-repetition totals per level. On the main task, correct answers below l6 numbered 2/30, 4/30, and 0/30 respectively; at l6 and above, all three scored 20/20. On this route the thinking-off trials fit within the context length through l5, so the gap noted in the previous paragraph is filled — on a separate route (fourteen errors occurred, all context-length overruns; they are recorded, and no condition was changed). The purpose of the repetitions was to compare the step against the spread: per-repetition totals ranged from 37,582 to 204,233 tokens within levels below l6, and from 2,734 to 5,258 at l6 and above. As descriptive statistics: the largest within-level spread observed was 2.7×, while the observed between-group separation was 22–33× — an order of magnitude larger. A by-product: the first repetition of each system steps on the same seeds as the reference runs and should, under identical conditions, reproduce them; in fact 1 to 8 cells of 20 matched, all cheap single-attempt cells. Measured quantities — token counts, attempt counts, outcomes — compare across hardware; token sequences themselves do not travel.

*A quarter of the capacity (reference route, single repetition).* Qwen3.5-2B — sharing the 9B's family, generation, and architecture at roughly 4.2× fewer parameters (2,274,069,824 against 9,653,104,368) — was run against the same inputs on the same hardware as the reference route, with one repetition. The boundary appeared at the same place. The step was 8.5× (69,670 → 8,220). Below l6 the model solved 1 cell of 12; at l6 and above, 6 of 8, taking the levels where code meanings are stated in the document (l6, l7) and the prose level (l9) in one or two attempts. At the one level where flag meanings sit inside the record (l8), it ran to the ten-attempt ceiling on both tasks without solving either, and its consumption fell back into the below-boundary band; the 9B solves this level with thinking on or off. There is a distance between information being present and a given model being able to read and use it; at this level, failure co-occurred with the lower-capacity configuration. Because that configuration also differed in reasoning behaviour — although THINKING=on was sent and not refused, the 2B emitted no reasoning text in any of the twenty trials, effectively running without thinking, which makes the 9B's thinking-off condition the closer comparator — and because it was measured with one repetition, the failure cannot be attributed to capacity alone. The 8.5× step sits above the 2.7× spread of the previous paragraph, but that spread was measured on different systems and different hardware, so it is not a direct yardstick.

*Single-shot distributions (separate instrument).* The retry-based method has two weaknesses that come from the method itself: the height of the step is set by the retry ceiling, and the outcome is binary. A separate instrument therefore drops retries, draws repeated single-shot responses under identical conditions, and reads whether the distribution of answers concentrates on the correct value; its implementation is incompatible with the retry-based series, and no direct numerical comparison is made. The design is a 15-cell grid: targets stated t (0–2) × distractors stated d (0–4); what is written determines the number of remaining hypotheses H = C(6−t−d, 2−t), and γ = 1/H serves as each cell's chance level. A 200-sample pilot was run first; the decision rules were then frozen in writing; the confirmatory measurement ran 200 samples × 2 repetitions (n = 400) on a different seed band from a single frozen commit. The pilot set the rules, so it is not pooled into the confirmatory estimates and appears only as an external reference. What held steady: the five cells of the t = 2 row (H = 1 throughout) came out identical across the pilot and both repetitions — correct-peak share 1.00, abstention 0.00 — and moving the peak threshold from 2% to 20% leaves this row at one peak. What did not: in the t ≤ 1 rows, cell-level values were not stable under independent repetition — the between-repetition |difference| in correct-peak share has median 0.10 and maximum 0.60 (t0_d4: 0.00 against 0.60); abstention-rate differences have median 0.08 and maximum 0.19; one cell exceeded γ+0.05 in each repetition, but different cells (t0_d1 at 0.56 in repetition 1; t0_d2 at 0.25 in repetition 2). The pre-frozen precondition for curve fitting — two or more points above γ+0.05 — was met in none of the four sections (t = 0 and t = 1, each repetition), and following the frozen rule, "no fit established" is recorded as a formal result. Abstention reads at two levels: the row-wise contrast (abstentions occur at t ≤ 1, none at t = 2) held across all three runs, while cell-level rates within t ≤ 1 move between repetitions and cannot be treated as stable at this sample size. What the instrument shows is that success under permitted retries and the concentration of single-shot distributions are different quantities. Reservations: no statistical tests were run; peak counts depend on the relative threshold (barely moving between 2% and 5%, dropping sharply from 10%); one model lineage, the same synthetic tasks, two repetitions; and that the cell-level terrain "does not hold steady" is not the same as "no structure exists."

**4.5 Deriving requirements from logs.** Section 6 develops, as policy, the idea of working backwards from operational logs to a requirements specification; against the reasoning text of 4.3, the derivation could actually be tried. The procedure has four stages: extract, from the reasoning of failed attempts, sentences stating that something is missing; group them by which field is said to be absent; write the groups out as requirements; then check, with the same extractor, whether mentions disappear at the level where that description was added. The extracted sentences read as requirements almost verbatim ("The problem is, the data doesn't list the industry type for each company."). After the final gate (described below), ten sentences survived: nine classified under the meaning of the industry codes, and one unclassified. Units, activity status, and resolving the external reference each came to zero — and the meaning of the industry codes is the only thing these two tasks actually require, so the derivation tracks what the task needs. The fourth stage served as its own check: mentions numbered 1–3 at l0–l5 and zero at l6–l9.

The procedure had to be rebuilt once, and the record is kept. In the first implementation, group headings and the sentences beneath them did not correspond, for two reasons: extraction was too broad — of 191 triggering sentences, 101 described a gap not in the data but in the *question* ("whether companies that have ceased operations should be excluded is not specified"), which is ambiguity in the task, not absence in the data — and grouping was decided by whichever keyword appeared first in a sentence. After adding a gate on the grammatical subject and scoring groups by match count, the ten sentences above survived: a yield of 5%, consistent with most of the search having been directed at the question's ambiguity rather than with over-strict extraction. The tasks here were built for an experiment, so this ratio against real operational logs is unknown; but the data side is evidently not the only source of expanding search, and the same logs should also yield what the question ought to have stated. A remaining caveat: the later attempts turn toward inferring what the questioner expects and generate no requirements, so a real-log application would need to use only what precedes that turn, and where to draw that line has not been measured.

**4.6 Traps on the measuring side.** Three records are kept because each contaminated, or would have contaminated, the indicator itself. First, a grader flaw: a defect in the number-extraction rule marked correct answers wrong, and the more a model wrote thousands separators the more it was penalised (verdict reversals ranged from 0 to 298 by model). A wrong verdict triggers a retry and inflates the denominator, so the quantity being measured was reflecting the grader; results in that state were discarded. Second, task escape routes: a task built to be unguessable turned out, twice, to have a way around it — first elimination, then the ordering of codes and the number of companies per group. Before those were closed, more capable models appeared to show smaller steps; once closed, that difference vanished. Seven identifiers have been enumerated and closed, and there is no way to show that no others remain. Third, a retraction: an earlier version of this work stated the thinking-off step as 62.7× (171,343 → 2,734). The numerator was a running total at the point a context-length error cut the trial off — not a valid measurement — and the figure was withdrawn; the symmetric thinking-off comparison was later obtained on the separate five-repetition route (32.1×, previous subsection), which is a separate route's figure and does not restore or replace the withdrawn one.

## 5. Limitations

The limitations below are stated once here, gathered from the measurements they belong to.

*What the indicator cannot read.* This measurement cannot answer whether lighter models benefit more from self-description. The step sizes across systems (23.4 / 29.3 / 14.5 / 30.2 / 25.5 / 8.5) do not follow model capability; they track the length of the response at the ceiling, because the step is a ratio of "ten attempts against one" and is therefore determined by the ceiling setting and the response length. The hypothesis that lighter models benefit more is not refuted but unanswerable by this design; a different indicator is needed. That the same model, on the same unsolved trial run to the same ten-attempt ceiling (l2), consumed roughly 1.8 times as much with thinking off as with it on is consistent with response length contributing materially to the ratio, though a capability effect cannot be separated from it.

*Coverage of the token accounting.* Reasoning-token counts are available only for the locally served runs; the three API systems returned `reasoning_tokens` as zero and no reasoning text, so there is nothing to count, and the tokeniser-recount approximation of 4.3 cannot be applied to them either. Cache efficiency (L3 in Section 3.2) was not measured.

*Scope of the tasks and data.* All measurements use two synthetic toy tasks. Whether the same shape holds for real data, or for other tasks, has not been measured; the transfer across tasks, models, and real data hypothesized in Sections 3 and 6 remains untested. The training side, and the question-framing side, of the self-description hypothesis were not measured. On the task-design side, seven identifying clues have been enumerated and closed, and there is no way to show that no others remain (4.6).

*Repetition and environment.* The five-repetition measurements ran on hardware different from the reference route, so within-level variance on identical hardware has not been obtained; the quarter-capacity system was measured with one repetition, and its variance is unknown. The single-shot instrument used one model lineage and two repetitions, and no statistical tests were run anywhere in this paper — shares were placed next to chance levels, and steps next to spreads, as descriptive statistics only. That the instrument's cell-level terrain does not hold steady under repetition is not the same as showing that no structure exists; separating the two requires further measurement with more repetitions or larger per-cell samples.

*Outcome measure.* The numerator of the RoT measurements throughout is the minimal binary form: whether the task was solved. The single-shot instrument measures a different quantity — the concentration of answer distributions — as an independent instrument, and its results are not numerically integrated with the retry-based RoT series. Richer outcome measures connecting RoT to value-added statistics remain unstandardised (3.1).

## 6. Policy implications

The implications below are stated as policy hypotheses, conditional on the transfers that Section 5 records as unmeasured; none of them is a validated rule.

*From standards conformance to log-derived requirements.* Adopting RoT as an indicator opens a way to shift how data preparation is steered: from conformance to a standard defined in advance, toward requirements read from measured operation. The log of recursive tokens an agent spent filling missing context — the reasoning that ran long, the retries that repeated, the definitions it queried — can, read backwards, provide evidence for candidate requirements about what the data ought to state. Section 4.5 reports a first trial of this derivation on the toy tasks; whether it works against real operational logs is untested. If it does, publishers gain an empirical instrument: instead of asking only whether data conforms to a metadata standard, they could read from operation what consuming systems failed to find — and, as the same trial suggests, surface ambiguities in the questions posed to the data that call for clarification.

*A candidate heuristic for investment priority.* If reference counts and per-reference reductions can be measured in operation, they compose a candidate prioritisation heuristic: investment priority = number of references × tokens saved per reference. We state this as a policy hypothesis, not a validated rule. The reduction figures entering it were measured on this paper's tasks, and whether they carry over unchanged to other tasks or to real data has not been measured; reference counts would themselves need to be measured consistently in the relevant operational setting. If the heuristic survives testing, it would complement existing high-value-dataset determination (Section 2): legal lists and systematic reviews enumerate categories ex ante, and a consumption-based axis would let measured operation revise priorities ex post. National master data — corporate registries, addresses, place names, statutes — sit at heavily referenced junctions and would be natural first test sites, not confirmed priorities.

*Open data as a digital public good.* Under RoT, the evaluation of open data gains a candidate axis besides publication counts and downloads: how much recursive computation is reduced, per use, in the inference processes that consume a dataset. To the extent that such per-use reductions transfer beyond the tasks measured here and the dataset is used repeatedly, a one-off description cost on a non-rival good would yield a cumulative benefit across those uses. Who should bear the one-off cost remains open (Section 3.3): the structure of complementary investment implies under-provision if left to the market, and where downstream benefits are difficult for the party making the description investment to capture, some form of shared or collective provision may be worth considering — but this paper does not settle the assignment.

*Digital sovereignty, briefly.* If self-described data lets lighter open models complete tasks they otherwise could not — for which Section 4 records one single-point measurement and one observed limit, and Section 5 records that capability-dependence cannot be read from this design — then a path would open toward completing more tasks in local or lightweight environments, with reduced dependence on large external proprietary models. We note this as a possibility consistent with the measurements, not a demonstrated capability.

## 7. Conclusion and call for collaboration

This paper proposed Return on Token — outcome obtained per total tokens invested, recursive and discarded search included — and examined the hypothesis that RoT varies with the self-description of data. On two synthetic tasks, a boundary in consumption appeared where required meanings entered the document, at the same position in every system measured on both retry-based routes; a first derivation of candidate data requirements from failed-attempt reasoning also ran. What remains unmeasured is gathered in Section 5, and the readings of Section 6 are policy hypotheses conditional on transfers not yet tested. We invite collaboration on precisely those transfers: reproduction on real government datasets, token accounting across model families, indicators that can read the interaction with model capability, and the question-framing side of the hypothesis. The measurement framework, task data, and full run records are published under the archived release described in the Data availability statement, and issues and pull requests are open.
